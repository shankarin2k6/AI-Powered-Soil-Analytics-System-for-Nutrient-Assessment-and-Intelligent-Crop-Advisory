WHY WE CHOSE EFFICIENTNET-B0 AND XGBOOST
AI-Powered Soil Analytics System - Team C
(Simple explanation for the presentation)

=====================================================================
0. THE ONE-MINUTE VERSION
=====================================================================

Our app has two different kinds of input, so we use two different kinds of model:

  1. A soil PHOTO  ->  image model  ->  EfficientNet-B0
  2. Lab NUMBERS (N, P, K, pH, ...)  ->  table model  ->  XGBoost

Rule we followed: use the model type that is known to work best for that kind
of data, and keep it small enough to run on an ordinary server without a GPU.

  - EfficientNet-B0 gives very good picture accuracy with only about 5 million
    parameters, so it is small, fast on a CPU, and does not overfit easily on
    our small soil dataset.
  - XGBoost is the standard winner for table data with few columns. It is
    accurate, trains in seconds, and can explain every answer (SHAP).

Both are also easy to explain to a farmer or an examiner, which was important
for a project about advice.


=====================================================================
1. WHY EFFICIENTNET-B0 FOR THE SOIL PHOTO
=====================================================================

1.1 What the job is
-------------------
Look at a photo and say which soil type it is (Alluvial, Arid, Black,
Laterite, Mountain, Red, Yellow) or "Not soil". This is image
classification. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are the normal tool.

1.2 What EfficientNet is, in simple words
-----------------------------------------
Older networks became better just by becoming bigger (more layers, more
parameters). EfficientNet found a balanced way to grow a network: make the
depth (layers), width (channels) and picture size bigger together, in the
right proportion. EfficientNet-B0 is the smallest, base version.
Result: good accuracy for very little size and computing cost.

1.3 Why it suits our project
----------------------------
(a) Small and fast. About 5.3 million parameters and about 0.4 GFLOPs per
    photo. It runs on a normal CPU in well under a second. We do not need to
    rent a GPU server to run the app.

(b) Works well with little data. Our soil dataset is small (about 1,000
    original photos plus augmented ones). Huge networks memorise small
    datasets and then fail on new photos. A small network that starts from
    ImageNet pre-training (transfer learning) learns the useful part (the
    colour and texture of soil) and overfits less.

(c) Pre-trained weights are available. We started from a network that already
    knows edges, textures and colours from millions of ImageNet photos, and
    only taught it soil. This needs far less data and training time.

(d) Soil is about colour and texture, not complex shapes. We do not need an
    enormous model to tell red soil from black soil.

(e) Works with Grad-CAM. We can draw a heat-map on the photo showing where
    the model looked. This lets us check that it looked at soil and not at a
    shoe or a plant. It helps us trust the model and explain it.

(f) Small file, easy deployment. The model file is only a few tens of MB, so
    it loads quickly and fits on a phone-friendly backend.

1.5 What we did to make it reliable (not just "pick a model")
------------------------------------------------------------
- Added a "Not_Soil" class and trained with hard negatives (photos of rooms,
  sky, people, bark, cloth, gravel) so the app does not give a soil name to a
  random photo. We compared model versions and chose the one that rejected
  non-soil best (model D).
- Used confidence levels: 60% or more = show the answer; 40% to 60% = ask the
  farmer to confirm; below 40% = ask for a new photo.
- Used Grad-CAM heat-maps to show what the model looked at.
- Checked results with a confusion matrix, not only accuracy.

2. WHY XGBOOST FOR THE LAB NUMBERS (FERTILISER ADVICE)
=====================================================================

2.1 What the job is
-------------------
Given a few numbers (N, P, K, pH, moisture, organic carbon, salinity) and a
few choices (soil type, crop, growth stage, season), suggest the fertiliser.
This is a classification problem on a small TABLE of data.

2.2 What XGBoost is, in simple words
------------------------------------
XGBoost builds many small decision trees one after another. Each new tree
tries to fix the mistakes of the trees before it. Adding all the small trees
together gives a strong, accurate model. It is called "gradient boosting".

2.3 Why it suits our project
----------------------------
(a) Best type of model for table data. For rows and columns of numbers, tree
    boosting methods like XGBoost usually beat deep neural networks, which
    are better for pictures, sound and text.

(b) Works with small data. Our fertiliser data has a few thousand rows at
    most. Neural networks need a lot more data to be stable. XGBoost is
    comfortable with this size.

(c) No heavy preparation. It does not need the numbers scaled or normalised,
    handles mixed columns well, and is not upset by columns on very different
    scales (pH 0-14 next to nitrogen 0-140).

(d) It can explain itself. With SHAP (TreeSHAP) we can show, for every
    suggestion, which values pushed the model towards the fertiliser or away
    from it. For example: "low nitrogen pushed the answer towards urea".
    A farmer or an agriculture officer can check whether the reasoning makes
    sense. For an advice system this matters more than a tiny accuracy gain.

(e) Fast and light. Trains in seconds, predicts in milliseconds, runs on a
    CPU, and the saved model is small.

(f) Built-in protection against overfitting. Settings such as tree depth,
    learning rate and number of trees, and cross-validation, let us control
    it.

(g) Easy to retrain. When better data arrives, we just train again and replace
    the model file. No change in the app.

2.5 What we did to make it honest
---------------------------------
- Showed a warning when an input is outside the range the model learned from.
- Showed "the model is not very sure" when confidence is low.
- Marked SSP as a hint only, because SSP was the weakest class in testing.
- Combined it with simple, readable lab rules (low / fine / high) so the
  health score and diagnosis do not depend on the model alone.


=====================================================================
3. WHY TWO MODELS AND NOT ONE BIG MODEL
=====================================================================

- There is no public dataset with a photo AND lab numbers for the same soil.
  One model cannot learn from two kinds of data that were never collected
  together.
- So each model does the job it is best at, and a small set of readable rules
  combines the answers: the soil type from the photo becomes an input to the
  fertiliser model, and the lab pH is used to check the photo's soil type.
- This is easier to test, easier to explain, and easier to improve one part at
  a time.


=====================================================================
4. LIKELY QUESTIONS AND SHORT ANSWERS
=====================================================================

Q: Why not ResNet-50, it is more famous?
A: It is five times bigger and ten times heavier to run, but not more accurate
   than EfficientNet-B0 on the standard benchmark. With about 1,000 photos the
   bigger model is more likely to overfit.

Q: Why not a Vision Transformer?
A: Transformers need very large datasets. With a small soil dataset a
   pre-trained CNN is the safer and cheaper choice.

Q: Why not deep learning for the lab numbers too?
A: For small tables, tree models like XGBoost usually match or beat neural
   networks, train faster, and can explain their answers.

Q: How do you know the image model is not just guessing?
A: We tested with a confusion matrix, added a Not_Soil class, trained with
   hard negatives, and use Grad-CAM to see where it looked. Low confidence
   asks the farmer to confirm or retake the photo.

Q: Can farmers trust the fertiliser advice?
A: It is a guide, not a lab report. The app shows the reasons (SHAP), warns
   when inputs are unusual, and tells the farmer to check with an agriculture
   officer before large purchases.

Q: What are the weak points?
A: The fertiliser data is probably synthetic, SSP is the least reliable class,
   most test photos came from the internet and not phones, there is no gravel
   class yet, and the confidence values are not calibrated. Real phone photos
   and field data are the next improvement.

Q: Can you change the models later?
A: Yes. The app loads the model files from one folder. A better model can be
   dropped in without changing the app.
